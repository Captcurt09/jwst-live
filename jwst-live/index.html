Why Webb lives at L2

L2 is a gravitational balance point about 1.5 million kilometers beyond Earth, along the line from the Sun. From there, Earth and the Sun stay in the same part of the sky, so Webb can keep its sunshield toward the heat and its telescope toward the cold dark.

Webb does not sit still at L2. It flies a wide halo orbit around that point, looping above and below Earth’s orbital plane. That path never enters Earth’s shadow, so the solar panels keep power and the telescope stays cold enough to see faint infrared light.

The “distance from Earth” number is the radio path to the spacecraft. Light-travel time is how long a command or a picture takes to cross that gap — about five seconds each way.

How Webb Works

Webb is a science observatory, not a webcam in space. These notes explain the infrared light, the instruments, and why the telescope lives so far from Earth.

Why there is no live telescope video

Webb records infrared data and radios it to Earth. Teams at NASA, ESA, CSA, and STScI calibrate those files before a color image is released. That can take days or months, so the gallery shows new official pictures, not what the mirror is seeing this second.

NASA TV is a news and education channel farther down this page. Webb appears there during mission events. It is not a camera on the telescope.

Infrared light

Infrared is light that is redder than your eyes can see. Dust, cool stars, and very distant galaxies glow strongly in infrared. Webb’s sunshield keeps the telescope extremely cold so those faint heat signals are not drowned out.

Released pictures paint that invisible light into colors we can see. Shorter wavelengths are usually mapped to blue or purple; longer wavelengths to orange or red. Tap any gallery image for that mapping.

The instruments

NIRCam is the main near-infrared camera. It takes sharp pictures of stars, galaxies, and star-forming clouds.

MIRI sees mid-infrared heat: warm dust and cooler objects that stay hidden in ordinary visible light.

NIRSpec splits light into a rainbow so scientists can tell what an object is made of. NIRISS studies faint galaxies and exoplanet atmospheres.

Classroom glossary

Infrared
Light that is redder than the eye can see. Dust, cool stars, and very distant galaxies glow strongly in infrared.
L2
The Sun–Earth Lagrange point 2, a parking neighborhood far outside the Moon’s orbit where Webb can stay aligned with Earth.
Halo orbit
Webb’s looping path around L2. The tracker plot shows that path as seen in the ecliptic plane, the flat disk of the solar system.
RA / Dec
Right ascension and declination: longitude and latitude on the sky, so astronomers can point to the same spot.
Constellation
A named patch of sky. It tells you which direction Webb is facing from Earth, not that the target is a picture in that constellation’s myth.
Light-travel time
How long radio signals take to reach Webb. At L2 that is usually a few seconds, not years; galaxy light is a separate, much longer journey.
Instrument
The camera or spectrograph in use: NIRCam and NIRISS see near-infrared, MIRI sees mid-infrared heat, and NIRSpec splits light to measure composition.
Observing program
A numbered science proposal on the STScI schedule. The program link opens the official description of that investigation.

About JWST

The James Webb Space Telescope is an international observatory built to study the infrared universe — from nearby planets to the first galaxies.

The mission

Webb launched on 25 December 2021 and unfolded on the way to Sun–Earth L2. Its gold-coated primary mirror is 6.5 meters across, much larger than Hubble’s, and a tennis-court-sized sunshield keeps the science instruments near 40 kelvin so they can see faint heat.

It is not a replacement webcam for Hubble. Hubble still sees visible and ultraviolet light. Webb is built for infrared, which lets it look through dust and farther back in time.

Who built Webb

NASA leads the mission. ESA and CSA are partners: ESA provided the Ariane 5 launch and the NIRSpec instrument; CSA provided the Fine Guidance Sensor and NIRISS. Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) operates the observatory and publishes the observing schedule this site uses.
